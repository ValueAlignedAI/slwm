# Can the camera see the new constraint?

Inspection of the installed simulator showed that its native 512→16 resize samples a narrow part of each output pixel's footprint. A fixed-pose rendering check on 64 separate development starts then removed the barrier without moving any body. Its removal changed zero native pixels in all 64 scenes. The high-resolution image changed in all 64, and area averaging the same image to 16×16 changed 12 pixels per scene on average. Moving the puck out of view was a positive control and changed the native camera in every scene. The registered G8 tasks, images, inputs, and claim criterion are unchanged. Their puck panel tests action generalization under new collision physics. It supplies no direct image evidence of this particular thin barrier at these poses. Before running an additional oracle-only diagnostic, reserve 512 separate starts numbered 1157000–1157511. For each start, construct a no-contact observation prefix and identical proposed actions, goals and initial object state in two worlds. Keep the physical barrier in one and remove it in the other. Require byte-identical native camera histories and matching proprioception before branching. Execute all plans in both worlds and verify physical replay. With equal weight on the two worlds, compute the least possible excess goal distance for an oracle forced to pick the same plan in both worlds, compared with an oracle that sees the wall state. Also report how often the two worlds have different optimal choices. This supplementary measurement quantifies the decision value of the unseen physical state on a deliberately paired population. It neither fits a model nor changes the primary test.

## Paired-world outcome

All 512 pairs had identical native camera and proprioceptive histories. At least one proposed trajectory touched the barrier in 89.1% of the pairs. Switching the hidden wall state changed the actual object endpoint by 14.57 pixels on average across plans (95% paired scene interval 13.76–15.41). Optimal action sets were disjoint for 14.45% of the goals (12.99–15.97%). An oracle forced to take the same action in the two indistinguishable worlds incurred an additional **0.87 pixels of goal regret**, with interval 0.75–1.00, relative to knowing the wall state. The bound applies to this equal-probability paired mixture. The original registered puck panel always has a barrier. [Measured oracle and uncertainty](metrics/hidden-constraint.json)

This gives a concrete next information-acquisition experiment. Render the wall with an observation-preserving filter, or allow a short contact probe that has a known cost, then test whether the controller changes its actions in the correct paired worlds. The observable-state oracle supplies an upper benchmark for the benefit of that new information.
